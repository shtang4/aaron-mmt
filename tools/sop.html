1 Scan the market Diagnose

  1. Heatmap. Mostly green → long bias. Mostly red → short bias. Mixed → no trades today.
  2. Hot sectors → the leading coin in each. Then Top Movers (24h, and the 15m tabs). Volatility and volume are the must; +4% in two hours is too slow. A gainer is a candidate, never a trade. Weekends: volume dries up; widen the search or stay flat.
  3. Screener, scored out of 4: EMA dominant direction across 1D · 4H · 1H · 15m · 5m · 1m (one dissenting frame does not veto) · OI line tilting up at the right end · funding sign counter to your side · depth lighter on your own side (bid thinner for a long). 4/4 full size · 3/4 at 50–75% with a tighter stop · below 3, no trade for a beginner (session: intermediates 2/4, advanced 1/4, at own risk). Funding settles 8 AM, 4 PM, midnight MYT.
  4. Exception, day-1 / day-2 listings only. The multi-timeframe EMA check is impossible (the daily has two candles) and funding and depth are uninformative on a new book, so the screen is EMA direction + OI direction only — OI on Bybit's OI Data tab at 5m. "That's good enough." Expires once the coin has real higher-timeframe history. Never applies to a coin with history; 2/4 is his advanced tier, so beginners skip newborn coins instead.
  5. News-driven pump — four questions first (ZETA, 23 Mar 2025): who pumps (exchange volume breakdown; one venue above ~50% is that venue's retail flow — real while it is awake, gone when it sleeps) · what news, and when · where is the liquidity, both sides, both models · how late am I versus the call. A trade call is a limit-on-retracement instruction, never "buy now".
  6. Sentiment gauge is a clue, not a signal. Greed ≠ buy.

2 Set direction Diagnose

  1. EMA 155 rule, on the Heikin-Ashi chart: three consecutive candles with the body above and no lower wick → long only; body below and no upper wick → short only. Nothing else sets direction. Order prices still from real candles.
  2. Heikin-Ashi run with no trend-side wicks confirms. Dojis or small-body clusters → wait 2–3 candles; same colour after = congestion, trend continues; colour change = the turn.
  3. EMA fan width is trend strength. Bunched lines = weak; spread fan = confirmed. Lines repeatedly crossing back and forth = ranging, skip.
  4. Retracement test, once you are in — three levels of severity. EMA 9/25/55 tangling with each other while all three stay above EMA 155 = noise, hold. Three full-body candles past EMA 155 = the warning that direction has gone. The fan fully inverted (255 > 155 > 55 > 25 > 9) = the confirmation: "cut loss or exit". On another EMA set, read the equivalent pivot — 155 is a role, not a magic number.
  5. Donchian ribbon (period 20) solid in the same colour confirms. Mixed → skip. The ribbon colour is a BOS proxy: a pullback that is only a CHoCH leaves the colour alone, a CHoCH followed by a BOS flips it. Colour holds = retracement; colour flips = the structure broke. Sensitive on 1m.
  6. SMC, the one entry trigger it gives. Equal lows at a strong low → CHoCH → BOS = long ("pump like crazy"). Equal highs → CHoCH → BOS = short ("dump very hard"). All three, in that order. The sweep itself is the abnormal-volume bar with the long wick.
  7. Seahorse — a wait, not a skip (session). 1H shows the shape (vertical move done, then a flat "beak"); drop to 15m for the beak's red Heikin-Ashi candles. Do not enter inside the beak. Draw the line across it, wait for the breakout, then long. Beak length = length of the wait. The stop must not sit under the beak's low — those equal lows are where the sweep goes.
  8. Hockey stick (slow decline, sharp V, vertical blade): most fail. A turn that does not cross EMA 155 is a retracement inside a downtrend and continues down — that is a short setup, not merely a skip. Only a cross plus three full-body candles above EMA 155 is the long, and even then you wait for the retrace to enter. Three full bodies + the three fast lines turning up = a BOS, not just a CHoCH. Below EMA 255, "the last barrier", is full breakdown. Never buy the blade.

3 Sniper entry Diagnose · SPE

  1. TradingView: nearest demand zone below (long) or supply zone above (short), and any FVG. Take the near edge: top of demand, bottom of supply.
  2. Bybit: EMA 9 is the first target; if the zone edge sits deeper, the EMA closest to the edge. That is the price. Any of the five lines is parkable — 9 fills most often, 155 is the deep one with the pivot behind it, 255 is "the last guard". Trail the order up the line.
  3. Rest a limit order there. Re-place it if the EMA moves. Limit is the default; a market order needs a reason — "don't enter as a market order unless some critical condition". Never market-buy the candle that is running. Wait at the lounge.
  4. Correlated alts making the same move at the same minute are one trade. Size the total, not each ticket.

4 Size and stop Dose

  1. Isolated margin. Leverage 1× to 5× as a beginner, 1× to 10× once past beginner (session). Capital you can afford to lose. Low leverage is not safety: liquidation sits about 100 ÷ leverage % away (5× → 20%, 25× → 4%). The stop caps the loss; leverage only sets its room.
  2. Margin per trade: $20 to learn; then 1% of wallet; never above 10%. Loss at the stop never above 1–2% of equity, whatever the score.
  3. Check the heatmap before placing the stop. A cluster just beyond it gets swept before the move you want — move the stop past it, or lower the leverage. A cluster is a magnet, never support: price is drawn in and the forced orders accelerate through it. KL to Penang: the biggest band is the destination, but a smaller band on the other side is a detour often taken first — "it depends on the money maker". Size the stop for the detour. Re-read it before any re-entry: once price reaches a cluster it is consumed, and the next destination — and the tradeable direction — flips to the other side. A double top is the symptom; the emptied band is the cause.
  4. Stop as ROI on margin, by tier: beginner 5–20%, intermediate 20–50%, advanced 50%+. Convert to price; it must sit beyond the structural level (far edge of the zone, or the heel low). If it does not, lower the leverage until it does. Never move the level, never widen the tier.
  5. Risk first, margin second. Ask "how much can I afford to lose if I'm wrong" before picking a margin. margin × leverage = position size; P&L = margin × ROI. You control margin and leverage completely, the price move not at all — a bigger margin multiplies whatever ROI arrives, including a negative one.
  6. Late entry → smaller dose. Early with time to manage it → scale bigger, partials, trail. Late with the move already running → small size, quick trade. Lateness is a sizing input, not only a warning.
  7. Never copy anyone's position size. A call gives you a price, never a size — that number comes from your wallet and your tolerance.
  8. Write the money at risk in USDT before entry. If the number makes you flinch, the dose is wrong. Size on capital, experience and emotional tolerance — never on confidence. "Do you decide your position size based on how confident you feel?" That is the whole fix for exiting winners early.

5 Exit plan, both ways Discharge

  1. TP first. Wick Tracker is a Bybit TP trigger at a level a wick will reach: slightly above the prior wick high (a sweep aims at the stops resting beyond it), the supply-zone bottom, the nearest large liquidation cluster (rank by the dollar figure, not the brightness), or 50% ROI raised to 55–60% for fees. Trigger on Last; inside the wick you expect, above the wick already made.
  2. Two regimes, decided by the fan. Fan widening, no two lines crossing → no fixed target needed: ride it on the climax rule and the EMA cross, Wick Tracker resting only as the unattended backstop. Fan flat, bunched or crossing → set the target and take it.
  3. SL from step 4, trigger on Mark, resting before the entry fills — the protective stop, never optional, isolated or cross. Both orders in before you look away.
  4. Then the Hunting SL replaces it once in profit, on the profit side of entry. Two stops, in that order — "when you profited, only then you set stop loss" means replace, not start. Keying it in: a percentage in Bybit's SL field is read as a loss (type 20 → −20% ROI). Compute the price entry ± entry × locked ROI ÷ leverage and enter that. Manual, re-keyed each time. Once the trigger is on the profit side, being stopped out is the take-profit — a "profitable loss" (ATA: SL hit at +339%).
  5. Climax exit: a volume bar above both VOLMA lines ("abnormal" — session-defined, no multiple) + the MACD histogram at its mountain peak, confirmed when the next bar prints shorter — on Bybit that bar is drawn hollow → close 75%. Move SL on the runner to entry.
    Abnormal volume with a long wick is a liquidity sweep, not a move; with a full body it is real. Red bar = selling, green = buying.
  6. MACD is two shapes. Iceberg dip (deepest red) times the entry, mountain peak (tallest green) is the exit; a short reverses them. Miss the peak → half the profit gone at the zero line, all of it back at your entry, then a loss. Cannot sit through a give-back? Trade dip-to-peak and stop there.
  7. Runner: Hunting SL locks a rising ROI, entry ± entry × ROI ÷ leverage. EMA 9 crossing back through EMA 25 is the backstop for the runner only — on a full position, the fast lines crossing each other is noise while all three sit above EMA 155.
  8. The TP is a plan, not a promise. "Most of the time it never hits the TP." Watch it; take the move if liquidity offers it early. The stop stays untouched.
  9. Be flat or already paid around the funding settlements — 8 AM, 4 PM, midnight MYT. Midnight is the one he names for manipulation.
  10. Stop hit = the plan worked. Take it. Nothing replaces it.
  11. Take the written level — not sooner, not never. ZETA is his own proof: the Wick Tracker at 2.8530 fired 1.5 cents under the high, and the coin was at 0.045 two years later. "Never exit will liquidate."

6 After the close Follow up

  1. Sweep realised profit from Derivatives to Funding.
  2. Close and go. Next coin. Never re-enter the same beak. Nothing is held past 24 hours.
  3. Stop at the daily target or ten trades, whichever first. Rest.
  4. Log it in the journal: which D, which rule, what you would change. Thirty trades before you believe any number.

7 When it goes wrong Discharge

  1. The stop is the answer. It was written before entry; honour it.
  2. If you skipped the stop and are underwater: close it. The loss is the loss.
  3. If you want the opposite direction: close the loser first, open the new trade as its own sized position with its own stop. Never both legs at once.
  4. The one hedging case: a position opened in cross mode by mistake. Hedge to freeze exposure, then close it or switch it to isolated, then close the hedge.
  5. Why hedging is not a general alternative to the stop — his own terms: cutting loss is "easy", hedging is "way troublesome" and "very stressful", best case "at least break even" = zero. You trade a small, known, pre-sized loss for a stressful process whose best outcome is nothing, paying fees and funding on two legs. If a stop feels unaffordable the position is too big: fix Dose, not Discharge.
  6. News, FOMC, black-swan candles: no method is 100%. Smaller dose, or flat.

Never, because the course's own risk module says so

  • Cross margin. Liquidation takes the whole wallet.
  • A position without a resting stop. "Set it before the entry order fills."
  • Hedging instead of taking the stop. The only hedge is on an accidental cross-mode position, and it ends with closing it.
  • Holding a loser to breakeven. Upside zero, downside the account — STORJ reached −1881% that way.
  • Trading a mixed heatmap. Entering inside a seahorse's beak — the breakout is the trade, not the chop.
  • Entering on a chat call without the chart check. "If you don't know the setup, don't take the shot."
  • Shorting a pump that is still running. "No one can tell where the pump will get."
  • Holding for days. This is not swing trading.
  • New listings beyond a few days; no EMA 155 to lean on.
  • Adding to a losing position to push liquidation away. "Against the money": a 100 loss becomes 500.
  • Blaming mindset before checking which D you skipped.
